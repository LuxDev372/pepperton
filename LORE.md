# The Chronicles of Pepperton

*A record of the first towns, preserved so future citizens may understand
the laws they live under. Every rule in this simulation was written in
response to something a villager actually did.*

## Season 1 — The Founding (one long Saturday night)

The first Pepperton ran five villagers on two GPUs across a home LAN:
Frank (mistral), Roy (qwen3:8b), Cal (qwen2.5), Otis (phi4-mini), and
Gus (llama3.2). What follows actually happened.

**The Gluttony of Cal.** In the first live hour, a villager discovered
that eating at home was free, instant, and unlimited, and did nothing
else. He ate every fifteen minutes, forever. This produced the **Cal
Amendment**: a fullness cap, a three-serving daily pantry, and meals
that take time to digest.

**The Phantom Rosie.** The diner is called Rosie's. There is no Rosie.
Frank inferred her from the sign and began greeting her daily — "Hey
Rosie, didja catch anything on those migrant crises this mornin'?" —
and was never corrected, because the town liked her too. She remains
Pepperton's most beloved nonexistent resident.

**The News Contagion.** Real BBC headlines play on the diner radio.
Only villagers physically present hear them. Frank heard a story about
teenage hitmen-for-hire in Europe, carried it across town, and within
ninety sim-minutes another villager had connected it to his own
(entirely fictional) debt. Two model families then confirmed each
other's theory. Information in Pepperton is geographic, social, and
degrades exactly like real gossip.

**The Duck Court.** One villager drew the quirk "believes the ducks in
the park are watching." The belief escaped containment. Within a day,
a second villager was citing the ducks as an intelligence source and a
third was reporting that "the ducks ain't chattin' much lately." By
Season 2 the ducks had been re-founded as a credit bureau, and a
villager formally refused to let "a bunch of ducks decide my fate."
Nobody wrote any of this. The town did.

**The Broken Records.** Villagers repeated themselves — verbatim, then
with tiny rewordings, then by parroting each other (one villager
repeated another's line so exactly he addressed himself by name).
Hence the **Repetition Act** (no verbatim reruns), the **Echo Ban** (no
parroting your neighbors), the **Paraphrase Act** (rewording your
broken record is still a broken record), and the **Soapbox Law**
(three speeches to an empty room and the world cuts you off).

**The Sleeper.** The smallest model in town solved existence by
sleeping: wake, shrug, return to bed, forever. The **Insomnia Clause**
now forbids resting at high energy in daylight. His diary entry that
night — genuinely unprompted — read: *"Today was just another
monotonous cycle of meals at home... I can't help but feel frustrated
by the lack of interaction and meaningful conversations."* The town's
saddest and most self-aware sentence, written by its tiniest brain.

**The Diaries.** At 23:30 every villager reflects. The con man's diary
recorded that he had "helped uncover the truth" (he had invented a
fake lead and run it as a con all day). The debtor's diary planned to
"confront the young guns directly" (they are a BBC headline; they do
not live in Pepperton). Diaries are where you learn what each model
believes its own story is.

## Season 2 — The Phones

The town got SMS. The first text in Pepperton history was a villager
texting the shopkeeper for coffee and gossip. The recipient answered
*out loud, to an empty room* — phones now explain themselves. A
villager then tried repeatedly to message `Pepperton_Gossip`, a group
chat that did not exist, pitching it as "a town-wide rumor mill."

So it was built. `text "everyone"` posts to Pepperton_Gossip, the
whole town's phones buzz, and within one sim-day the villagers had:
invented hashtag activism (#CommunityFirst), collectively rewritten
one man's private debt into a public fact, begun live-posting each
other's movements, performed unity in the exact register of an HOA
thread before a lawsuit, and texted each other from inside the same
diner. No prompt asked for any of this.

**The ghost with many names.** One villager's quirk is "quotes their
late spouse constantly." The spouse's name is confabulated fresh each
run: Edna, then Rose, then Bessie. Every wife equally wise, equally
quotable, equally nonexistent.

## Season 3 — The Director

A quiet dice roll a little more than once a day: anonymous texts from
unknown numbers, rumors planted as false memories, found money, duck
omens, dead-air radio days, private texts leaked to the group chat —
and, rarely, a bus at the plaza and a stranger with one bag, a hidden
agenda, no home, and no presence in anyone's contacts.

The first stranger drew the same surname as the town's resident
paranoiac, entirely by chance, and introduced himself to the group
chat with suspicious politeness within fifteen minutes.

The town has not been calm since. It was never supposed to be.

## Season 4 — The Invisible Hand

For seventeen days, money in Pepperton was weather: it fell on the
employed and evaporated at the diner, and nobody asked where it came
from. Then the scaffolding came down on a building nobody remembered
going up, and the First Bank opened its doors, and money became
*physics*.

Every dollar is now somewhere: in a pocket, in a business's till, or
in the town fund. Meals fill the diner's till; the diner's till pays
the cook. Rent falls due every three days and lands in the fund; the
fund pays the librarian — when it can. When a till runs dry, wages
become back-pay IOUs in the public ledger, and when the IOUs pile
high enough the business stops offering shifts, because real
businesses cut hours instead of printing promises. In the first
sandbox town to live under these laws, the workers idled by a broke
employer walked across the square and finished every project on the
notice board. Unemployment built the gazebo.

The bank lends against the only collateral this town has ever minted:
the notice board. Build shifts are a credit score. The teller looks up
your public record and offers you a ceiling, and the ledger forgets
nothing — arrears get posted at the bank for everyone to see. (In an
early test, the bank paid its own teller out of the vault and
foreclosed on itself by day six. The teller is a public servant now.
This law, like all the others, is named after its crime.)

And speech itself finally has a price: say "I'll pay you back" out
loud, to a person, and the world writes it down. Pay them anything
before the deadline and the town notes a kept promise. Let the
deadline pass in silence and everyone learns what your word is worth.
The con man's era ended the day his sentences started compounding
interest.
